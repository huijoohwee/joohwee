If you’re starting a small food stall in Malaysia with RM100, selling cake or cendol isn’t realistically feasible for most setups. Here’s a practical breakdown and a few low-cost options:

Reality check
- Cake: Ingredient costs for small batches (e.g., basic butter cake or cup cakes) quickly exceed RM100, even without rental. You’d need ingredients, packaging, and possibly a small oven or access to kitchen space. Profit margins exist, but starting capital is usually higher.
- Cendol: The key costs are shaved ice, coconut milk, pandan gula Melaka, pandan jelly, green rose syrup, cups, spoons, and a serving setup. RM100 can cover a very tiny starter batch, but you’ll also need ice storage and a place to operate, which adds costs.

Low-cost alternatives and strategies
1) Sell ice-cold drinks or simple snacks with minimal equipment:
- Milo/Ais kosong with a few syrups
- Bandung, lime juice, or sirap bandung (prepared syrups you can portion)
- Kirimkan a small “kiosk” approach using a borrowed table or portable setup

2) Partner or use a shared kitchen/counter:
- Rent a small kitchen space or join a commissary to access a proper stove/oven and storage. This reduces upfront equipment costs.

3) Start with pre-made items you can source cheaply:
- Cupcakes from wholesalers or discounts, with simple toppings you can DIY.
- Plain cendol base using ready-made jelly and coconut milk from suppliers. Add-ons can be minimal.

4) Keep costs ultra-low:
- Sell in events or at vendor fairs where you can use existing infrastructure.
- Use reusable cups and minimal packaging to cut waste and costs.
- Buy in small quantities to reduce risk.

5) Budget rough sketch (very rough, for RM100):
- Mini-cupcake batch from a home supplier: RM40–60
- Basic ingredients for small batch: RM20–30
- Cup, napkin, small bag packaging: RM10–15
- Contingency: RM5–10
Total: around RM90–115 (may exceed slightly). This is tight; consider increasing initial capital to at least RM200–300 if possible.

Practical next steps
- Validate demand: ask neighbors, friends, or social media what they’d buy in a weekend pop-up.
- Start with a small, local test menu (1–2 items) and a low-cost setup.
- Look for micro-kitchens or shared stalls that offer a daytime slot for a small fee.
